# Selling Beyond Your Borders? Five Website Fixes That Help Foreign Buyers Say Yes

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

A procurement manager in Nairobi needs a new packaging supplier. A buyer in Accra is looking for an engineering firm in Johannesburg. A distributor in Europe wants a second source of supply in Lagos. Before any of them sends an email, they will look at the supplier's website, and often decide there and then whether to make contact at all.

This is not a guess. In 6sense's 2025 Buyer Experience Report, B2B buyers first contacted a seller about 61% of the way through their buying process, and 94% had already ranked their shortlist before they spoke to any vendor. For a business selling across borders, the website is the first meeting, and the buyer usually holds it without you.

Most company websites are built for customers who already know them. A foreign buyer does not. Here are five fixes that help a website answer the questions a buyer from another country asks first.

## 1. Say where you deliver, and on what terms

A buyer abroad wants to know quickly whether you can serve them at all. Put it on the page: the countries you deliver to, typical lead times, minimum order quantities and the delivery terms you work with. If you only serve some markets, say so. A clear "no" saves both sides a week of emails.

Be specific about what happens at the border. Say who handles customs clearance, which documents you supply with each shipment and how goods are packed for long journeys. These are the details a buyer's logistics team will ask about first, and answering them on the page shows that you have done this before.

## 2. Explain how your pricing works

Many suppliers hide prices because every order is different. That is understandable, but a buyer comparing three suppliers will often contact the one that gives them something to work with. Show price ranges where you can, state the currencies you invoice in, and explain what changes the price: volume, specification, packaging and freight.

## 3. Make contact easy from another country

Write phone numbers in full international format, with the country code. Offer the channels your buyers actually use, which may include WhatsApp alongside email and phone. Show your time zone and your business hours, and say how quickly you reply. Then keep that promise. A fast, useful first reply can win a buyer who has been waiting on a competitor.

Keep the enquiry form short. Ask for what you need to reply properly: the product, the quantity, the destination country and a contact. A form that demands a full company profile before a buyer has even seen a price will lose many of the people who were ready to talk.

## 4. Prove that you are real

A buyer who has never met you is looking for reasons to trust you. Make them easy to find:

- Your registered company name and registration details
- Certifications, with the issuing body and the date
- Real photographs of your premises, people and products, not stock images
- The names and roles of the people a buyer will deal with
- Examples of your work and references, where your customers have agreed to them

Make the key documents easy to download: product specification sheets, test reports and certificates. A buyer who has to email you just to see a certificate may simply move on to a supplier who publishes theirs.

Each item answers a doubt before it becomes a reason to choose someone else.

## 5. Help search engines show the right page to the right buyer

If you have pages for different countries or languages, search engines need to know which page is meant for whom. [Google's guidance on localised versions](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions) recommends hreflang annotations: each language or country version should list itself and every other version, using full web addresses, with an "x-default" page for visitors who match none of them.

Two common mistakes are worth avoiding. Google advises against changing content automatically based on the visitor's IP address, partly because most of its crawling comes from the United States, so it may never see your other versions. And Google ignores location meta tags, so they will not do the job on their own.

## Measure enquiries by country

Once these fixes are in place, record the country of every enquiry and every order. After a few months you will see which markets find you, which ones enquire, and which ones buy. That tells you where to translate next, where to advertise and where your website still falls short.

## Where to start this week

You do not need a new website to begin. Ask someone outside your company, ideally in another country, to spend ten minutes on your site and write down every question it leaves unanswered. Fix the first five. That short list is usually the best guide to what a real buyer is missing.

Cross-border trade has always depended on trust. Today much of that trust is built, or lost, on a website before the first conversation happens.
