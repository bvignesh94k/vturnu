# Sample Requests: The Early Demand Signal Textile and Apparel Exporters Can Track

*An order book shows what buyers decided months ago. A simple sample register shows which markets, channels and products are moving now.*

By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU

In textiles and apparel, an export order rarely arrives straight after the first email. Between the first enquiry and a confirmed order there is usually a sample stage. The buyer asks for swatches or hangers, then lab dips or strike-offs, then a proto or fit sample. Only after that does a purchase order follow, often weeks or months later.

Most exporters track orders carefully. Samples are often tracked loosely, in email threads, courier receipts and the memory of a merchandiser. That is a missed opportunity. A sample request is the earliest firm sign that a buyer is considering you. If every request is recorded in one place, you can see where demand is forming long before it shows up in the order book.

This article explains how to set up a sample register, which four numbers to review each month, and which decisions those numbers can support.

## Key Takeaways

- A sample request is the earliest firm sign of buyer interest, yet many exporters do not record requests in one place.
- Count development samples (swatches, hangers, lab dips, strike-offs, proto and fit samples), not approval samples that follow an order.
- One shared sheet with fixed columns and a fixed list of eight to ten sources is enough to start.
- Four monthly numbers guide decisions on fairs, portals, markets and products: requests by market and source, sample-to-order rate, days from sample to order, and sample cost per order.

## Which samples to count

Count development samples: the samples a buyer asks for before placing an order. These include fabric swatches and hangers, lab dips, strike-offs, and proto or fit samples.

Leave out the approval samples that follow an order, such as pre-production (PP), top of production (TOP) and shipment samples. They matter for quality, but they tell you about orders you already have, not about new demand.

Also mark whether each request comes from a new buyer or an existing one. A repeat buyer asking for a new season's lab dips is good news, but it is a different signal from a new buyer in a new market.

## Set up a sample register

One shared spreadsheet is enough. Use one row per request, and keep the columns fixed so the data can be counted later.

| Column | What to record |
|---|---|
| Date requested | The date the buyer asked |
| Buyer | Company name, and whether it is a new or existing buyer |
| Market | The buyer's country |
| Source | Where the buyer first found you (from a fixed list) |
| Product | Quality, article number or product group |
| Sample type | Swatch, hanger, lab dip, strike-off, proto or fit |
| Dispatched | Date sent, plus the sample and courier cost |
| Feedback | Date and result: approved, revise or dropped |
| Order | Order date and value, if one followed |
| Reason lost | Price, quality, lead time, compliance, no reply or other |

The Source column needs the most care. Agree on a short, fixed list of eight to ten channels before you start: for example, each trade fair by name, B2B sourcing portals, your website, email campaigns, agents, LinkedIn, and referrals from existing buyers. If one merchandiser writes "website" and another writes "Google", the numbers cannot be added up.

To fill the Source column honestly, ask every new buyer at first contact how they found you, and write down what they say. For website enquiries, use tagged links (UTM parameters) in your email signatures, portal profiles and trade fair material, so your analytics can separate these visits from the rest. Google's free Campaign URL Builder creates these links. A QR code on your stand that points to a tagged link shows how many visitors from that fair went on to look at your website.

## Fill in the last three months first

You do not have to start from zero. Courier invoices and dispatch records list every sample sent, with dates and destinations. Match them against email threads to fill in the buyer, product and source. Three months of history gives you a baseline in the first week.

## Four numbers to review every month

**1. Sample requests by market and source.** This is the volume of new interest. A rise in requests from a market you have not targeted is worth a closer look. A fair that produced many conversations but few sample requests may not deserve the same budget next year.

**2. Sample-to-order rate by source.** Divide the number of samples that led to an order by the number of samples sent. Measure it only for samples old enough to have had a fair chance: for example, those sent more than 90 days ago, or whatever your usual lead time is. Some channels bring many requests and few orders. Others bring fewer requests from more serious buyers.

**3. Days from sample to order.** Track the median number of days between dispatch and order, by product group. It tells sales when to follow up and helps production plan ahead. When a sample has had no feedback well past that usual time, a polite check-in is due.

**4. Sample cost per order.** Add up the cost of making and sending samples for each source, and divide it by the orders that source produced. Development samples can be expensive, especially for small runs sent by express courier to distant markets. This number shows which channels cost the most to serve.

## Decisions the register supports

After three to six months, the register answers questions that are usually settled by instinct:

- **Trade fairs:** which fairs led to sample requests and orders, not only to conversations at the stand.
- **Portal listings and advertising:** whether a paid listing or campaign brings sample requests from the buyers you want.
- **Markets:** which countries move from samples to orders, and which stall at the sample stage.
- **Products:** which qualities attract requests but lose on price or lead time. That points to a costing or capacity question rather than a marketing one.
- **Sample policy:** whether to charge for samples above a certain value, or credit the cost against the first order, in channels where few samples turn into orders.

The Reason lost column becomes valuable here. If many samples to one market are dropped for compliance reasons, such as a certification the buyer requires, that is a business decision to make, not a sales failure.

## Common mistakes

- **Recording only the samples that worked.** The dropped samples hold most of the lessons.
- **Changing channel names halfway through.** Fix the list at the start and add a new channel only when you need one.
- **Mixing development and production samples.** The numbers then rise and fall with your order book, not with new demand.
- **Judging a channel too early.** A sample sent last month has not had time to become an order. Compare like with like.
- **Keeping the register in one person's inbox.** Share the sheet between sales and merchandising, and review it together once a month.

## Start this week

Create the sheet, agree on the source list, and fill in the last three months from courier records. Then add every new request on the day it arrives.

An order book tells you what buyers decided. A sample register tells you what they are deciding now, and where your next orders are likely to come from.

## FAQs

**Should sample requests from existing buyers be counted?**
Yes, but mark them separately from new buyers. A repeat buyer's request shows continued business; a new buyer's request shows new demand.

**How long before the numbers become useful?**
Three months of history, rebuilt from courier records, gives a first baseline. Comparisons between channels become dependable after three to six months, depending on your usual lead time from sample to order.

**Is special software needed?**
No. A shared spreadsheet is enough to start. Moving the register into a CRM makes sense once the volume or the team grows.
