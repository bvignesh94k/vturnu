# Before Marketing Presses Send: The Campaign Brief Every Contact Centre Should Ask For

*A one-page brief, sent before launch, lets planners schedule for the extra demand and gives advisors the answers customers will ask for.*

By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU

Many contact centres learn about a marketing campaign from the queue. Calls, chats and emails rise on a Tuesday morning, and advisors start hearing about an offer they have never seen. Customers quote an email the team has not read. Some ask about terms nobody can confirm. Others arrive from a landing page that tells them to "call now" without saying why.

This is rarely anyone's fault. Campaigns are planned in channels, audiences and send dates. Contact centres plan in intervals, skills and shifts. The two plans seldom meet, because nobody owns the handover between them.

A short, standard campaign brief fixes most of this. It takes little time to complete, and it gives planners and advisors what they need before the first contact arrives.

## What the brief should contain

Keep it to one page, in the same format every time, so planners always know where to look.

**1. What is going out, to whom and when.** The channels (email, SMS, paid social, direct mail, website banners), the audience and its size, the send dates and times, and whether sends are staggered by region or segment. A staggered send spreads demand. A single send to the whole customer base concentrates it.

**2. The offer in plain words.** What the customer gets, who qualifies, the start and end dates, exclusions, and how to redeem. Include the exact wording customers will see, because that is the wording they will quote back.

**3. Where customers will land.** The link to the landing page and its main call to action. Note whether the page shows a phone number, a chat button or a form, and whether it tells customers to call. A page that says "call us to claim" will create calls. A page that answers the obvious questions may prevent them.

**4. Expected response and likely reasons for contact.** Marketing's estimate of how many people will respond, and what the estimate is based on, such as a similar earlier campaign. Then the likely reasons customers will get in touch: eligibility questions, codes that do not work, delivery dates, cancellations.

**5. An advisor FAQ.** The five to ten questions customers are most likely to ask, each with an approved answer, plus who to escalate to when a question is not on the list.

**6. Codes and tagging.** Promotion codes, CRM campaign codes, and a wrap-up or disposition code for contacts about this campaign. Without a tag, nobody can say afterwards how many contacts the campaign created.

**7. Who can change or pause it.** Named contacts in marketing, with their working hours, and an agreed rule for when a send can be slowed or paused if queues or service levels come under pressure.

## Send it early enough to plan

A brief is only useful if it arrives before schedules are fixed. Agree on a lead time that suits your scheduling cycle: for example, ten working days for large campaigns and less for small ones. Ask marketing to send an update whenever the offer, audience or dates change, and to flag any last-minute send straight to the planning team.

A simple rule helps: above an agreed audience size, no brief means no send. It sounds strict, but it protects customers from long waits and protects the campaign from complaints that undo its good work.

## Use past campaigns to forecast the next one

Marketing's response estimate is a starting point, but your own history is better. Pick a previous campaign of a similar type and size. Plot contacts by interval for the week after the send, and tag them by reason if your system allows. This shows how quickly demand built, when it peaked and how long it lasted.

Keep a small library of these curves by campaign type: email offers, price changes, product launches, policy letters. Over time, planners can scale the right curve by audience size instead of starting from a guess.

Website data can give an early warning too. If marketing shares the landing page's visitor numbers during launch day, planners can see within the first hour or two whether interest is running above or below the estimate, and adjust breaks, overtime or callbacks before queues build.

## Close the loop after every campaign

Within a week or two of launch, hold a short joint review with marketing:

- **Volume:** how many contacts the campaign created, compared with the estimate.
- **Reasons:** the top reasons customers got in touch, from the tags and from advisor feedback.
- **Avoidable contacts:** contacts caused by unclear terms, broken links or missing information on the landing page, which better copy could prevent.
- **Experience:** service levels, abandonment and customer comments during the campaign period.
- **Next time:** one or two changes to the brief, the landing page or the send plan.

Share the cost side too. When marketing sees how many contacts a campaign created and why, the next email can answer more questions before customers need to ask them.

## Make it routine

A brief only works when it becomes a habit. Put the template where marketing already plans its campaigns. Keep a shared campaign calendar that planners can see. Name one owner on each side. And thank marketing when a good brief keeps the queues calm, because a quiet launch day is easy to take for granted.

The contact centre does not need to approve every campaign. It needs to hear about each one in time to prepare. One page, sent early, is usually enough.
