# Did That Coverage Work? Three Website Checks Any Communicator Can Run

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

A story about your company runs in a respected publication. The team shares the link, leadership is pleased, and then someone asks the question communicators dread: did it do anything?

For years the stock answer was advertising value equivalency, the cost of buying the same space as an ad. The measurement body AMEC rejected that answer as far back as 2010, when its Barcelona Principles stated that AVEs are not the value of communication. The principles have been updated since, and the direction has not changed: measure outcomes, not column inches.

Most communicators agree in theory. In practice, outcome data can feel like someone else's department. It does not have to be. Your company's own website already records what people did after they read about you. Here are three checks you can run with tools your marketing colleagues already use.

## 1. Did more people search for your name?

Coverage rarely sends every reader straight to your site. Many remember the name and search for it later. Google Search Console, a free tool, shows how often your pages appeared in Google for each search term. Ask whoever manages the website to filter for searches that contain your company or product name, then compare the two weeks after the story with the four weeks before it. A rise in branded searches is a clear sign that the coverage reached people.

## 2. Did readers arrive, and where did they land?

Open your web analytics and look at two numbers for the same period. The first is referral visits from the publication's domain. These are readers who clicked a link in the story. The second is direct visits, which can rise when people type your address after seeing your name.

Then look at the landing pages. If most new visitors arrive on the home page and leave, the story created interest that the site did not catch. That is worth knowing before the next announcement.

## 3. What did they do next?

Attention is not an outcome. Look at what the new visitors did: newsletter sign-ups, demo requests, job applications or views of the investor page, whatever matters for that story. A funding announcement should move the careers and investor pages. A product story should move trial or contact requests.

If your inquiry form asks "How did you hear about us?", read the answers for the weeks after the coverage. Sometimes people name the article.

## Three habits that make the numbers credible

- **Write down the baseline before the story runs.** A comparison made afterward, from memory, convinces nobody.
- **Note what else happened in the same weeks.** If an ad campaign or an event ran at the same time, say so. Claim what the data supports and no more.
- **Run the same three checks every time.** One story proves little. Twelve stories measured the same way show which topics and outlets move people.

None of this needs a new platform or a budget. It needs a standing 20-minute meeting with the person who runs your analytics, and a one-page summary after each major story. If you want the reasoning behind the first check, I have written about [branded and non-branded search and what each one tells you](https://vturnu.com/blog/search-visibility-vs-revenue-demand/).

Communicators have long argued that earned media changes what people think and do. The website is where part of that change leaves a trace. It is worth looking.
