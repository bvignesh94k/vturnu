# Consent Is Now a Design Problem: What India's Data Protection Rules Mean for Every Lead Form

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

Open almost any business website in India and find the enquiry form. Name, phone number, email, a message box and a button that says Submit. Somewhere near the button, in small grey type, there is often a line saying that by submitting you agree to the privacy policy and to being contacted. Sometimes a box has been ticked for you.

That small grey line is about to become one of the more important pieces of design on the page.

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified in November 2025. They come into force in stages, and the main duties for businesses, including how consent is asked for, take effect in May 2027. That is about six months away. Most of the conversation so far has been about lawyers and policies. I think much of the real work will be done by marketers and designers, one form at a time.

The standard the Act sets for consent is short enough to memorise. Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, with a clear affirmative action. It should cover only the data needed for the stated purpose. And a person must be able to withdraw it with the same ease with which it was given.

Read those lines again with a lead form in front of you, and several familiar habits stop working.

A box that is ticked in advance is not a clear affirmative action. The person did nothing. One tick that covers the enquiry, the newsletter, WhatsApp messages and sharing with partners is not specific. Each purpose needs its own choice. A line such as "we may use your information to improve our services" is not informed, because it tells nobody what will actually happen. A brochure that can be downloaded only after agreeing to marketing calls is hard to describe as free or unconditional. And a form with twelve fields, when three would answer the enquiry, collects more than the purpose needs.

The withdrawal rule deserves special attention. If someone subscribed with one tap, an unsubscribe process that needs a login, a phone call or five screens is not comparable. Many marketing teams have never tested their own opt-out on a phone. It is worth doing this week.

There is also a question of language. The Act expects a notice to be clear and plain, and to be available in English or in the Indian languages listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution. A notice written by a lawyer for other lawyers will not meet the spirit of that, whatever language it is in.

None of this is bad news for marketing. I would argue the opposite.

A database built on vague consent is full of people who never wanted to hear from you. They do not open emails. They block numbers. They pull down every measure a marketer is judged on, and they cost money to store and contact. I have always believed that marketing should be measured by qualified leads, conversion and revenue, not by the size of a list. A shorter list of people who gave a clear yes is worth more than a long list of people who said nothing.

So what should a marketing head do in the next six months? I would start with four things.

First, list every form. Not only the contact page, but the newsletter box, the event registration, the chatbot, the WhatsApp button and the landing pages from old campaigns that nobody switched off.

Second, for each field on each form, write down in one line what the data is for. If you cannot, the form should not ask for it.

Third, rewrite the notice in the words your customers use. One or two plain sentences near the button, saying what you will do with the details and how to make it stop.

Fourth, make leaving as easy as joining, and keep a record of what each person agreed to and when. If consent is ever questioned, it is the business that will have to show that notice was given and consent was taken.

There is a temptation to treat all of this as a compliance cost and hand it to the legal team. I think that would waste an opportunity. The enquiry form is often the first conversation a brand has with a customer. Asking clearly, for only what is needed, and accepting no for an answer has always been good manners. The law now agrees.

This is a marketer's reading of the rules, not legal advice. Please check the details of your own case with a qualified adviser.
