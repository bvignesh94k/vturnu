# Is Your Website Fast Enough for Your Customers? Three Numbers Every Business Should Check

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

Most business owners judge their website on a laptop in the office, on a fast connection. Most of their customers meet it somewhere else: on a mid-range phone, on mobile data, between other tasks. On that phone, a page that takes too long, freezes when tapped or jumps around while it loads loses people before they read a word.

Speed is not a matter of taste. Google measures it with three numbers called Core Web Vitals, and anyone can check them for free. Here is what they mean, where to find them and what usually fixes them.

## The three numbers

[Google's guidance on Core Web Vitals](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals) gives a target for each:

- **Largest Contentful Paint (LCP)** measures loading. The main content of the page should appear within 2.5 seconds.
- **Interaction to Next Paint (INP)** measures responsiveness. When someone taps a button or a menu, the page should respond in less than 200 milliseconds.
- **Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS)** measures stability. The layout should not jump around as the page loads, and the score should be less than 0.1.

These are not abstract scores. A slow LCP is a customer looking at a blank screen. A poor INP is a customer tapping "Call us" twice because nothing happened the first time. A high CLS is a customer pressing the wrong button because the page moved.

## Where to see them

- **Google Search Console** has a Core Web Vitals report that shows which pages pass or fail, based on visits from real users.
- **PageSpeed Insights** lets you test any page. Look first at the field data from real visitors, then at the lab test, which explains what is slowing the page down.
- **An ordinary phone.** Open your most important pages on a mid-range phone using mobile data, not office Wi-Fi. It is the simplest test there is, and often the most revealing.

## What usually slows a business website down

In my experience, the same few causes appear again and again:

1. **Oversized images.** A photo straight from a camera can be many times larger than the page needs. Resize and compress images before uploading them.
2. **Too many plugins and scripts.** Chat widgets, sliders, pop-ups, tracking tags and social feeds each add weight. Remove anything nobody uses.
3. **Hosting that is too far away or too cheap.** If your customers are local, a server on another continent adds delay to every request. A content delivery network can help.
4. **Heavy fonts and animations.** Several font families and decorative effects make a page slower to show and slower to respond.
5. **Pages built to impress, not to inform.** A homepage that opens with a large video can look good in a meeting and still lose visitors on a phone.

## Who should own it

Speed falls between chairs. The developer built the site, the hosting company runs the server, and marketing adds the scripts. Give one person the job of checking the three numbers each month and the authority to ask for fixes. Check again after every redesign, new plugin or marketing campaign, because those are the moments when sites usually get slower.

## Start with the pages that matter

You do not need to fix every page at once. Start with the pages that bring enquiries and sales: the homepage, your main service or product pages and the contact page. Improving those few often does more for the business than any new feature.

A fast website does not win customers on its own. A slow one quietly loses them. If you want to go further, this [Core Web Vitals guide for business owners](https://vturnu.com/blog/core-web-vitals-guide-for-business-owners/) explains each fix in more detail.
