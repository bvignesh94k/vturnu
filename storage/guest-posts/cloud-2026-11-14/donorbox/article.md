# Redesigning Your Nonprofit's Website? How to Keep the Donors and Search Traffic You Already Have

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

A new website is supposed to help a nonprofit raise more, recruit more volunteers, and tell its story better. Too often, the first months after launch bring the opposite. Visits from Google drop, online gifts slow down, and the team starts to wonder whether the old site, for all its faults, was working better than anyone realized.

In most cases, the new design is not the problem. The problem is that pages that used to bring people in have moved or disappeared, and nobody told search engines, email subscribers, or partner websites where they went. The good news is that this is one of the most preventable problems in digital fundraising. It just needs a plan before launch day, not after.

This guide walks through that plan, step by step.

## Why nonprofit websites are especially exposed

Nonprofit sites tend to be large and old in a good way. Over the years they collect program pages, impact stories, event pages, annual reports, volunteer guides, and campaign landing pages. Many of those pages are linked from places you no longer control: partner websites, local news articles, old newsletters, social posts, and even printed materials.

That history is valuable. It is also exactly what a redesign can break. When an old page address stops working, every link pointing to it stops working too. Search engines gradually drop the page, and the people who followed those links land on an error page instead of a donation form.

## Step 1: Make an inventory before you change anything

Before the design team moves a single page, list every page on the current site that brings people in or helps them give. You need three sources:

- **Google Search Console** shows which pages appear in search, which search terms bring visitors, and which other websites link to you.
- **Your analytics tool** shows which pages people land on first and which pages come before a completed donation or volunteer sign-up.
- **Your own records** of the links you have published: email campaigns, social media, grant reports, partner pages, and anything printed with a web address or QR code. Printed materials are easy to forget, and they can stay in circulation for years.

Put everything into one spreadsheet with the old address, the number of visits, and whether the page leads to gifts or sign-ups. This becomes the most important document in the whole project.

## Step 2: Decide what to keep, merge, or retire

Go through the list and decide what happens to each page.

- **Keep and improve** pages that bring visitors, donors, or volunteers, even if they look dated. A refreshed version of a page that already ranks will usually do better than a brand-new page that starts from zero.
- **Merge** several thin pages on the same topic into one stronger page, such as five short event recaps into one page about your annual event.
- **Retire** pages that are truly obsolete, such as a campaign that ended years ago with no links and no visits.

Resist the temptation to cut content just because the new design has fewer menu items. A page can stay on the site, and keep its search value, without being in the main navigation.

## Step 3: Map every old address to a new one

For each page you keep, merge, or retire, decide where its visitors should land on the new site, and set up a permanent (301) redirect from the old address. Send each one to the closest match: the old "Ways to Give" page to the new giving page, an old program page to the new program page.

Avoid redirecting everything to the homepage. A supporter who clicked a link about your food program and lands on the homepage has to start searching again, and many will not.

Google's own guidance on site moves is clear on timing: keep redirects for as long as possible, generally at least one year, so search engines can transfer the old pages' signals to the new ones. For a nonprofit, there is a strong case for keeping them indefinitely, because old newsletters and printed materials keep sending people to old addresses long after launch.

## Step 4: Protect the donation path above everything else

Your donation pages are the most important part of the site, and they deserve their own test plan:

- Make a real test donation on the new site, on a desktop computer and on a phone.
- Check one-time and monthly gift options, dedication or tribute fields, and any campaign-specific forms.
- Confirm that receipts are sent and that the thank-you page appears.
- Check that completed gifts are recorded in your analytics, so you can compare results before and after launch.
- Make sure every old donation link, including those in recent emails and on social profiles, redirects to a working form.

Timing matters too. If you can, avoid launching a new site in your busiest weeks of the year, such as the run-up to year-end giving. A launch problem in a quiet month is an inconvenience. The same problem in your biggest fundraising week is expensive.

## Step 5: Watch the staging site, then check everything at launch

New websites are usually built on a hidden copy, and that copy is rightly blocked from search engines during the build. One of the most common launch mistakes is that the block travels to the live site. A single setting can tell search engines to ignore your entire new website.

On launch day, check that:

- The live site is open to search engines, the robots.txt file allows the pages you want found, and no important page carries a "noindex" instruction.
- Your most important old addresses redirect correctly, one by one.
- The new XML sitemap is submitted in Search Console.
- Analytics records visits, and every form, including donations, volunteer sign-ups, and newsletter subscriptions, actually sends.
- Pages load quickly on a phone. Google's Core Web Vitals give useful targets: the main content should appear within about 2.5 seconds, the page should respond to a tap in under 200 milliseconds, and the layout should not jump around as it loads.

## Step 6: Build accessibility in from the start

A redesign is the best moment to make your website easier for everyone to use, including supporters with disabilities. WCAG 2.2, the current version of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, added nine new success criteria. Two are especially relevant to donation pages: buttons and other targets should be large enough to tap (the standard sets a minimum of 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with some exceptions), and login or verification steps should work with password managers and copy-and-paste.

Accessible pages are also simply easier pages. Clear labels, readable contrast, and forms that work with a keyboard help every donor, not only some.

## Step 7: Update the links you control

Redirects catch old links, but it is better to update the ones you can reach yourself. After launch, go through the places your organization publishes its web address and point them straight at the new pages:

- Email templates, signatures, and automated welcome or receipt emails
- Social media profiles and pinned posts
- Your listings on charity directories and giving platforms
- Partner and sponsor websites that link to a specific program or campaign page

A short, friendly note to your most important partners asking them to update a link is usually welcome, and it saves their readers a redirect.

## Step 8: Watch the first eight weeks closely

Expect some movement in search results while Google recrawls the site. What you want to see is a dip that recovers, not a slide that continues.

Each week for the first two months, check three things:

1. Pages reported as "not found" in Search Console, and fix each one with a redirect.
2. Visits from search and online gifts, compared with the same weeks before launch.
3. The pages that lost the most visitors, and whether their content survived the move.

Most problems trace back to a missing redirect or a page that lost its content. Both are quick to fix once you know about them, and both are much harder to fix if you only notice them at the end of the year.

## A short checklist to share with your team

- Export your top pages, links, and donation paths before the design work starts.
- Keep and improve pages that bring donors, even if they look old.
- Redirect every old address to its closest new page, and keep those redirects.
- Test every donation form on desktop and mobile before launch.
- Avoid launching during your busiest giving weeks.
- Remove the staging block, submit the sitemap, and check tracking on launch day.
- Build in accessibility rather than adding it later.
- Review search traffic and online gifts weekly for eight weeks.

A redesign is a good moment to improve how your organization looks and works online. It should not be the moment you lose the supporters it took years to earn.
