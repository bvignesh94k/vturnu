# One Page, Five Readers: Designing B2B Service Pages for Buying Committees

**The person who finds a B2B service page is rarely the person who signs. Here is how to design one page that works for everyone who reads it.**

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

A manager has a problem and goes looking for help. They find a service page, like what they see and forward the link. Then the page meets everyone else: someone in IT, someone in finance, someone in procurement, and the people who will actually use the service every day.

Each of them opens the same page looking for different proof. Most B2B service pages are written for only one of them, usually the manager who found it. The page wins that first reader and quietly loses the people who decide.

## The committee reads the page before it talks to you

This is not a small group anymore. In [Forrester's State of Business Buying 2026](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forrester-2026-the-state-of-business-buying/), a typical purchase involved about 13 internal stakeholders and nine external influencers.

Most of that group never speaks to the supplier early. In [6sense's 2025 Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/b2b-buyer-experience-report/), buyers first contacted a seller about 61% of the way through their buying process, and 94% had already ranked their shortlist before they spoke to any vendor.

So the page has to answer the committee's questions on its own, without a salesperson in the room. That is a design problem as much as a copywriting one.

## Five readers, five first questions

Every committee is different, and your research should name the real roles. But in B2B service buying, five readers show up again and again, and each arrives with a different first question.

- **The champion**, the manager with the problem: "Will this fix my problem, and will I look good for choosing it?" They look for outcomes, examples from similar companies and a clear picture of how the work runs.
- **The budget holder**: "What will this cost, and what do we get back?" They look for a pricing approach, rough ranges, what is included and what drives the cost up.
- **IT and security**: "Is this safe, and will it fit our systems?" They look for how data is handled, where it is stored, who has access and what it connects to.
- **Procurement and legal**: "Is this supplier a safe choice?" They look for company details, contract terms, service levels, references and the steps to get started.
- **The people who will use it**: "Will this make my day harder?" They look for onboarding, training, support and what changes in their routine.

Most pages answer the champion well, answer the budget holder vaguely, and leave the other three to dig through PDFs or wait for a sales call.

## Find out who actually reads your page

Before redesigning anything, find out which of these readers you really have. Four sources are usually enough:

1. **One optional question on the enquiry form**, such as "What is your role in this decision?" Even a partial answer rate tells you a lot.
2. **Sales call notes.** Every question that comes up again and again after first contact is content missing from the page.
3. **Analytics paths.** The pages people view before they enquire, such as pricing, security or "about us", show which readers are already doing their homework.
4. **Short interviews with recent buyers**, both the ones who chose you and the ones who did not. Ask who else read the page and what they needed to see.

## Page patterns that serve the whole committee

Once you know your readers, a few patterns make one page work for all of them.

**Start with a plain summary.** Three or four lines at the top: what you do, who it is for, the outcome, and how long it takes. This is the part the champion copies into an email, so write it to be forwarded.

**Group proof by reader.** Short, clearly labelled sections such as "For IT and security" or "For finance" let each reader skip straight to their part. A busy CFO should not have to read three case studies to find out how pricing works.

**Write a short security and data section in plain words.** Say where data is stored, who can access it and how it is protected, then link to the detailed document for anyone who wants it.

**Give pricing guidance, even without a price list.** Ranges, typical project sizes and the factors that change the price do more for the budget holder than "contact us for a quote".

**Offer next steps other than "contact us".** A one-page summary to share internally, a sample deliverable, a technical FAQ or a pricing guide each lets a different reader take the next step at their own pace.

**Keep the detail in the page itself.** Accordions and tabs are fine for scanning, but the content should still be real text in the page, not locked inside images or downloads. Some committee members will never visit the page at all; they will read a colleague's summary, or ask an AI assistant to summarise it. Plain text in the page survives that journey far better.

If you are rethinking more than one page, this guide to [what to write on every page of a B2B website](https://vturnu.com/blog/what-to-write-on-every-page-of-a-b2b-website/) covers the rest of the buyer's path in the same spirit.

## Test the page with people who play each role

Usability tests for B2B pages often recruit one kind of participant and give everyone the same task. Committee pages need role-based tasks instead.

Give each tester a role and one question to answer using only the page:

- As the budget holder: "Find what this would roughly cost for a team of 50."
- As IT: "Find out where our data would be stored."
- As a future user: "Find out what the first month would look like for you."

Watch where they look first, how long it takes and where they give up. Then run a simple forwarding test: ask someone playing the champion to write the email they would send their boss, using only what the page says. If they cannot write it in two minutes, the summary is not doing its job.

After launch, measure more than form fills. Track enquiries by role, the questions that still come up on first calls, how often the shareable summary is downloaded, and how many enquiries turn into qualified conversations. A page that serves the committee should produce fewer repeated questions and better-prepared buyers, even if the number of form fills stays the same.

## Takeaways

- A B2B service page is read by a committee, most of whom never speak to you before the shortlist is set.
- Name your real readers first, using form answers, sales notes, analytics paths and buyer interviews.
- Lead with a summary written to be forwarded, then group proof by reader.
- Treat pricing guidance and a plain security section as content, not as things to hide behind a sales call.
- Test with role-based tasks, and judge the page by the quality of enquiries, not only their number.
