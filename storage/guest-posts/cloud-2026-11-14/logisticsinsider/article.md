# Shippers Search by Lane, Not by Slogan: What Logistics Websites Should Publish

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

A shipper looking for a new logistics partner rarely types "end-to-end logistics solutions" into a search box. They type the job. Reefer containers from Chennai to Rotterdam. A bonded warehouse near Mundra. Over-dimensional cargo from Pune to Chennai. Air freight for medicines that must stay between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius.

Then they open three or four websites and look for one thing: proof that this company does this exact job, on this route, with this kind of cargo.

Many logistics websites are not built for that visit. The home page promises seamless solutions. The services page lists ten modes in ten bullet points. The contact form asks for a name and a message. The shipper came with a specific question and leaves without an answer.

Buying logistics still runs on trust and relationships. But shippers now compare suppliers, book travel and track parcels online, and they bring the same habits to choosing a freight partner. For many of them, your website is the first meeting.

## What the shipper is checking

Before a shipper sends an enquiry, they are trying to answer a short list of questions:

- **Lane:** Do you move cargo between these two points, and how often?
- **Mode and equipment:** FCL, LCL, air, road or rail? Reefers, flat racks, low-bed trailers?
- **Cargo:** Can you handle hazardous, temperature-controlled, high-value or oversized goods?
- **Compliance:** Which licences and accreditations do you hold, and who issued them?
- **Facilities:** Where are your warehouses, and what can they store?
- **Time:** What is a realistic transit time, and what are the cut-offs?
- **Visibility:** How will I track the shipment and receive documents?
- **Response:** How soon will I get a quote?

A general services page answers very few of these.

## Five pages that answer those questions

**1. One page for each service.** Sea freight, air freight, warehousing, project cargo and customs clearance each need their own page. Say what you move, the equipment you use, the documents you handle and what the shipper must give you to begin.

**2. Lane pages for routes you run often.** If you run a weekly LCL consolidation from Chennai to Singapore, give it a page. Say how often it sails, the cut-off day, a typical transit range and the cargo you usually carry on it.

Build these only for lanes you actually serve. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) describe "doorway abuse": sites or pages created to rank for specific, similar search queries, which lead users to intermediate pages that are less useful than the final destination. Google's examples include "pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page" and "substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy". A page for every port in the world, with the same text and a new city name, looks like that. A page with the real schedule and details of a lane you run every week answers a real question.

**3. Facility pages.** For each warehouse or yard, give the address, the area, the storage types (ambient, temperature-controlled, bonded or hazardous), the handling equipment, any approvals specific to the site, the working hours and photographs of the actual site. A shipper choosing a warehouse wants to see it before visiting it.

**4. A credentials page.** List each licence and accreditation with the issuing body and the validity date. Examples include Authorised Economic Operator status under the programme run by Indian Customs (CBIC), IATA cargo agency accreditation and ISO certificates. A strip of logos is easy to ignore. A list with issuers and dates can be checked.

**5. A rate request form that asks what you need to quote.** Ask for origin and destination, cargo type, weight or volume, dimensions, ready date, Incoterms and any special handling. A form that asks only for a name and a message starts a round of emails before any price is given. A form that asks the right questions gets the shipper a quote sooner and helps your team see which enquiries are serious. Say next to the form how soon you will reply, and keep that promise.

If you are rewriting the whole site, this [page-by-page guide to B2B website content](https://vturnu.com/blog/what-to-write-on-every-page-of-a-b2b-website/) covers the other pages most B2B websites need.

## Write facts, not adjectives

"Seamless", "reliable" and "customer-centric" cannot be checked. "Weekly LCL to Singapore, cut-off Thursday" can. So can "temperature-controlled storage at 2 to 8 degrees Celsius, with logged readings" or "customs clearance at Chennai and Nhava Sheva".

Give transit times as ranges, with the reasons they vary: port congestion, customs holds, weather and transshipment. An honest range is more useful than a single promise that the shipper knows may not hold.

Rates change too often to publish. What a shipper can use is an explanation of what drives the price: the lane, the weight or volume, the equipment, the season and any special handling. Explaining this early helps avoid surprises when the quote arrives.

## Measure enquiries by lane

A page earns its place when it brings enquiries that turn into shipments. Record which page each enquiry came from. A hidden field in the form can capture this. Then count, for each page or lane, the enquiries received, the quotes sent and the shipments booked.

Patterns will appear. A lane page with many enquiries and few bookings may be attracting the wrong cargo, or setting the wrong expectation on price or transit time. A page with no enquiries may be about a lane few shippers search for. Review the numbers with the sales team every month and change one thing at a time.

## The first meeting

A shipper usually knows what they need before they search. The website's job is to show, quickly and clearly, that you do that job. A slogan cannot do this. A clear page for each service, lane and facility can.
