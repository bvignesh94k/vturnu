# Before Buyers Call, an AI Has Already Briefed Them: Five Questions for CEOs

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

The first conversation about your company now happens without you. A buyer asks an AI assistant to explain a market, compare suppliers and suggest who to call. The answer arrives in seconds, written with confidence, and it shapes the shortlist long before your sales team hears a name.

Most leadership teams have never seen that answer. They review pipeline, win rates and brand surveys, but not what the machines say about them when a buyer asks a simple question. That gap is now a leadership issue, and it deserves the same attention as any other source of revenue risk.

## The shortlist forms earlier, and out of sight

The research on business buying points in one direction. In [6sense's 2025 Buyer Experience Report](https://6sense.com/b2b-buyer-experience-report/), buyers first contacted a seller when they were about 61% of the way through the buying process. 94% had already ranked their shortlist before they spoke to any vendor, and the vendor they favoured before that first contact won about 80% of the time.

At the same time, the tools buyers use for that early research have changed. In research [Forrester published in January 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260121478240/en/Forresters-2026-Buyer-Insights-GenAI-Is-Upending-B2B-Buying-As-Leaders-Face-Mounting-Pressure-To-Justify-Every-Dollar-Spent), more than 90% of business buyers said they already use or plan to use generative AI to support purchase decisions. Forrester also found that 19% were less confident in a purchase because an AI tool gave them inaccurate or misleading results, and that buyers increasingly check what these tools say with peers, experts and the providers themselves.

Search itself has shifted too. Google said in July 2025 that [AI Overviews had passed 2 billion monthly users](https://digiday.com/media/googles-ai-overviews-reach-over-2-billion-monthly-users/). A [Pew Research Center study](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) of US browsing in March 2025 found that people who saw an AI summary clicked a traditional search result in 8% of visits, against 15% for those who did not. Google disputed the study's method, but the direction is hard to ignore: for many buyers, the summary is the first impression, and the visit to your website may never happen.

## Why this belongs on the CEO's desk

It is tempting to hand this to the marketing team as a new kind of search ranking. That misses the point. What an AI assistant says about a company draws on everything the company has made public: the website, case studies, directory profiles, reviews, press coverage, job posts and old pages nobody remembers publishing.

No single team owns all of that. Marketing owns the message, sales owns the conversations, IT controls which crawlers can read the website, legal owns the claims, and finance owns the numbers that end up in public. When nobody owns the whole picture, the answer buyers see is assembled by default.

The cost shows up quietly. A company that is missing from the answer is missing from the shortlist, and it never learns why. A company that appears with outdated facts spends the first sales call correcting them.

## Five questions to ask your leadership team

### 1. What do AI assistants say about us today?

Start with evidence, not opinion. Write down 20 questions a real buyer would ask, such as "Which firms help mid-sized manufacturers with X?" or "What does a project like Y usually cost?" Ask them in ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity and Google, and record three things each time: whether your company is named, whether the facts are right, and which sources the answer relies on.

Repeat the same questions every month so that the trend means something. If your team needs a starting point, here is [a simple method for measuring AI search visibility](https://vturnu.com/blog/how-to-measure-ai-search-visibility/) that any marketing team can run without new software.

### 2. Are our core facts public, consistent and current?

AI assistants repeat what they can find and confirm. If your website says one thing, a directory profile says another and an old press release says a third, the answer will be vague at best and wrong at worst.

List the facts that matter to a buyer: what you do, for whom, where you operate, how long you have done it, your certifications and how you price. Make sure each one is stated plainly on your own website, matches every public profile, and replaces the old versions rather than sitting beside them.

### 3. Is our proof easy to verify?

Forrester's finding that buyers check AI answers with peers, experts and providers is good news for companies with real proof. The question is whether that proof is public and specific. A case study that names the client, the problem, the work and the measured result is far easier to cite than a page of claims.

Ask how many of your strongest results are published in a form a buyer, or a machine, can check. Then ask what is stopping the rest, whether that is client permission, missing data or simply nobody writing them up.

### 4. Who owns the answer?

Give one person clear accountability for how the company appears in AI answers, with the authority to pull in marketing, sales, IT and legal. Their first technical check is simple: make sure the website's settings are not blocking the search and AI crawlers that feed these tools by accident. Their first editorial check is just as simple: retire outdated pages, policies and prices that still show up in answers.

### 5. How will we know it is working?

Agree on a small set of measures before anyone starts. Useful ones include the share of priority questions where the company is named, the share of answers with correct facts, and two questions added to every sales intake: "Which other companies are you considering?" and "Where did you first hear about us?" Track them monthly, next to pipeline, and look for movement over a quarter rather than a week.

## A leadership habit, not a campaign

None of this requires a large budget. It requires leaders to treat the company's public facts as an asset that someone maintains, the same way they treat financial reporting or brand guidelines.

The companies that do this well will not be the loudest. They will be the ones whose facts are clear, consistent and easy to check, which is exactly what an AI assistant needs before it puts a name on a buyer's shortlist.
