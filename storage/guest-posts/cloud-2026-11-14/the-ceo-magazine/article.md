# Stop Asking Marketing for More Traffic. Ask for These Four Numbers

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

Most CEOs see a marketing report once a month. It is usually full of activity: visits, impressions, followers, rankings and clicks. Most of these numbers go up most months. Revenue does not always follow.

The report is not wrong. It is answering a question nobody at the top asked. And when the question from the top is "can we get more traffic?", the team will deliver more traffic. People work toward the number they are asked for.

I run a digital marketing company, so I see this from the other side of the table. My view is simple: marketing should be judged by qualified leads, conversion rates, customer acquisition cost and revenue. Those are the four numbers I would ask for. Here is what each one tells a CEO, and what to listen for in the answer.

## Why this matters more when budgets are flat

Marketing budgets are not growing. In [Gartner's 2026 CMO Spend Survey](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-11-gartner-2026-cmo-spend-survey-finds-cmos-allocate-15-point-3-percent-of-marketing-budgets-to-ai-but-only-30-percent-are-ready-to-scale-ai-capabilities), marketing budgets averaged 7.8% of company revenue, against 7.7% the year before.

When the budget is flat, every new idea has to be paid for by stopping something else. A team can only make that choice well if it knows which work brings customers and which work only brings visitors. Activity numbers cannot tell them. These four can.

## 1. Qualified leads, with the definition in writing

Ask how many inquiries last month met the definition of a qualified lead. Then ask to see the definition. If nobody can produce it, that is your first finding.

Without a written definition, marketing counts every form that is filled in, sales counts only the ones worth a call, and both are right by their own rules. A one-page definition, agreed by both teams, settles most arguments about lead quality. It should say who the buyer is, what they need and what they have asked for.

**What to listen for:** a number that sales would agree with if they were in the room.

## 2. Conversion rate, by source

The second number is the share of qualified leads that become customers, split by where they came from: search, paid ads, referrals, events and email.

The split matters more than the total. A channel that sends half as many leads but twice as many customers deserves a bigger share of next quarter's budget. You cannot see that in a traffic chart.

**What to listen for:** whether the team can name the source of each customer at all. If the answer is "we cannot tell", the fix often starts with one field on the inquiry form and one in the CRM, not with a new platform.

## 3. Customer acquisition cost, fully loaded

Customer acquisition cost is everything spent on winning customers in a period, divided by the number of customers won. Everything means salaries, agency fees, software and advertising, not advertising alone.

On its own, the number means little. Set it beside what a customer is worth: the gross margin you expect from that customer in the first year, or over the life of the account. Then ask for the payback period in months.

**What to listen for:** a number that finance recognizes. If marketing and finance calculate it differently, ask them to agree on one method before the next meeting.

## 4. Revenue from marketing-sourced customers, with the delay stated

The last number is revenue from customers whose first contact came through marketing. Ask for it with the delay stated plainly.

If your sales cycle is six months, this month's revenue came from work done two quarters ago, and this month's work will show up two quarters from now. A report that ignores the delay will punish good work and reward luck. The honest way to show it is by group: of the leads that arrived in the first quarter, how many have closed, and for how much. For teams that want the detail, I have written a longer guide to [marketing attribution for long B2B sales cycles](https://vturnu.com/blog/marketing-attribution-for-long-sales-cycles/).

**What to listen for:** honesty about what cannot be measured. No method captures every influence on a sale, and a team that says so is easier to trust.

## One more question: what did we stop?

Four numbers tell you what is working. One question tells you whether the team is acting on it: what did we stop doing this month?

A team that never stops anything is not using its numbers. There is always something on the list that is not earning its place. Stopping it is how a flat budget pays for new ideas.

## How to introduce this without a fight

A change in what the CEO asks for can feel like a verdict on the team. It does not need to.

- Ask for one page with the same four numbers every month.
- Accept rough numbers for the first quarter. The gaps show what to fix in the data.
- Do not punish the first honest report. These numbers may look worse than the activity numbers they replace. That is information, not failure.
- Keep traffic on the page, but lower down. It is a useful early signal. It is not the result.

Expect one more thing. When a team starts chasing customers instead of visits, traffic sometimes falls while revenue rises. If that happens, the report is doing its job.
