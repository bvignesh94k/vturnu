# Sales and Marketing Argue About Lead Quality Because Nobody Wrote the Definition Down

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

Picture a monthly review that plays out in many companies. Marketing reports 300 new leads. Sales says only 20 of them were worth a call. Marketing says sales did not follow up. Sales says marketing sent names, not buyers.

Both teams are usually telling the truth. Marketing counts every form that was filled in. Sales counts the people who were ready for a conversation. They are measuring different things and calling both of them "leads".

The argument sounds like it is about quality. Most of the time it is about a definition that nobody wrote down.

## What a written definition changes

When the definition lives only in people's heads, "bad lead" is an opinion, and opinions are hard to fix. Once it is on paper, every disagreement becomes specific: which rule did this lead fail? That question has an answer, and the answer tells marketing what to change.

Here is how to set it up in three steps, and how to know whether it is working.

## Step 1: Write the definition on one page

Keep it short enough that a new hire can learn it in ten minutes. A useful definition answers four questions:

- **Fit:** Is this the kind of organization you sell to? Write rules a rep can check in a minute: industry, company size, region and the roles that usually buy.
- **Need:** Did the person describe a problem you solve, or ask about a product or service you offer?
- **Action:** What did they do? Asking for a quote, a demo or a call is a strong signal. Downloading a guide is interest, not a request. Decide in advance which actions count.
- **Timing:** If they mentioned when they want to act, note it. Timing can be unknown, but it should never be invented.

Then list what is not a lead: job seekers, students, vendors pitching to you and existing customers who need support. Each of them deserves a reply, but they belong somewhere other than the sales pipeline.

Finally, give the page an owner and a review date. Someone has to be able to say "this is the current version".

## Step 2: Add an acceptance step

A definition only works if someone applies it. The simplest way is an acceptance step: every lead that marketing sends must be accepted or returned by sales within an agreed time, such as one working day.

Returned leads need a reason, chosen from a short fixed list. For example:

- Outside our market (fails fit)
- No real need
- Could not reach them
- Duplicate or existing customer
- Spam or fake details

Keep the list short, and allow a comment for anything unusual. The point is not to score points against marketing. It is to collect evidence that marketing can act on. A lead that sits untouched for a week should be treated as returned, because nobody has taken responsibility for it.

## Step 3: Review the reasons every month

Set aside half an hour each month for both teams. Look at returned leads by reason and by source. Patterns appear quickly:

- If "outside our market" clusters on one campaign, the targeting or the message is attracting the wrong audience.
- If "could not reach them" is rising, check the form. Is the phone number optional? Is the first reply going out fast enough?
- If "no real need" comes mostly from one content offer, that offer is producing readers, not buyers. Keep it, but stop counting its downloads as leads.

Agree on one change per month and check the result at the next review. Small changes you can measure beat big changes you cannot.

## Two numbers worth tracking

**Acceptance rate by source:** the share of leads sales accepts, split by where they came from. A low rate means marketing is sending leads outside the definition, or sales is applying it more strictly than it is written. The return reasons will tell you which.

**Progression rate:** the share of accepted leads that reach the next stage, such as a first meeting, within 30 days. If acceptance is high but progression is low, the definition is probably too loose. Tighten the action or fit rules and watch both numbers.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Using a lead score instead of a definition.** A score is useful only if everyone knows what a given score means in plain words.
- **Changing the definition quietly.** If the rules change mid-quarter, last month's numbers no longer compare. Announce the change and date it.
- **Letting leads wait.** A lead that is neither accepted nor returned hides the problem. Make the time limit real.
- **Counting returned leads in marketing's total.** Report what was accepted. It is a smaller number, and it is the honest one.

## Treat it as an agreement

A written lead definition will not make every lead a good one. What it does is make every argument shorter and every fix more precise. Write it on one page, agree on it together and review it every month. The meeting that used to be about blame becomes a meeting about what to change next.
