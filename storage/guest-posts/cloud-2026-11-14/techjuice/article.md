# One Question on Your Signup Form Can Show Which Channels Really Bring Customers

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

Ask a startup founder where their customers come from and you will often get two different answers. The analytics dashboard says most signups came from "direct" traffic or organic search. The founder remembers that the last three good customers mentioned a LinkedIn post, a WhatsApp group and a friend's recommendation.

Both answers can be true at the same time. Analytics tools record the last click before a visit. They cannot see the conversation that made someone open a browser and type your name. When a visit arrives with no referral information, it is usually filed as direct traffic, which tells you very little.

There is a cheap way to see more of the picture: ask people. One question on your signup or demo form, answered in the customer's own words, can show you channels your analytics will never report. This approach is often called self-reported attribution.

## What to ask, and how

Add one question to the form people fill in when they sign up, book a demo or request a quote:

**"How did you hear about us?"**

A few details make the answers much more useful.

**Use a free-text box, not a dropdown.** A dropdown limits people to the channels you already know about. A text box lets them tell you about the podcast, the community group or the colleague you did not think to list. The answers are messier, but the mess is where the new information is.

**Keep it optional at first.** A required field adds friction to every signup. Start with an optional field placed after the essential ones, and watch how many people answer. If the response rate is low, test making it required on the demo form, where intent is higher.

**Place it close to the decision.** The demo request or paid signup is the moment that matters. A newsletter form that collects only an email address is the wrong place to ask.

**Keep the wording neutral.** "How did you hear about us?" works better than "Which of our ads did you see?", which suggests an answer.

## Turn messy answers into categories

Once a week, read every answer and sort it into a short list of categories. Ten or fewer is enough. For a B2B startup, the list might look like this:

- Search engine
- LinkedIn (post, comment or message)
- WhatsApp or community group
- Referral from a customer
- Referral from a partner or investor
- Podcast, YouTube or webinar
- Event or meetup
- Press or blog article
- Paid advertising
- Other or unclear

Keep the original answer next to the category. "My cousin uses your app" and "your founder spoke at an event in Lahore" are both useful details that a category alone would lose. Over time, the raw answers also give you the words customers use, which are often better than the words in your marketing copy.

## Compare it with what your analytics says

The value of self-reported data comes from setting it beside your analytics, not from replacing it. Put the two side by side for the same month:

| Channel | What analytics recorded | What customers said |
|---|---|---|
| Search | (count) | (count) |
| Direct | (count) | not an answer people give |
| LinkedIn | (count) | (count) |
| Community groups | rarely visible | (count) |
| Referrals | (count) | (count) |

The disagreements are the interesting part. If analytics shows a lot of direct traffic while customers keep naming LinkedIn or a community group, those channels are working harder than your reports suggest. If paid ads take a large share of the budget but almost nobody mentions an ad, ask why before you spend more.

Neither source is perfect. Analytics is precise about clicks but blind to conversations. Customers see the whole story but remember it imperfectly. Together, they give you a much better basis for a decision.

## Confirm it on the first call

For B2B startups with a sales or onboarding call, ask the question again in conversation: "By the way, how did you first come across us?" People often give a fuller answer when they are talking. Note it in your CRM or spreadsheet next to the form answer.

This second answer helps in two ways. It catches people who skipped the form question, and it often reveals more than one touchpoint. A customer may say they first saw a LinkedIn post, then heard about you again in a group, and finally searched for your name. That sequence is closer to how decisions are made than any single label.

## Use the data to make decisions

Self-reported attribution is only worth collecting if it changes what you do. Once you have a month or two of answers, look at three things:

1. **Which channels bring the customers who stay or pay.** If you can link the answer to the customer record, compare channels by revenue or retention, not just by signups.
2. **Which channels are missing from your plan.** A community group or a podcast that keeps appearing in the answers may deserve real time and attention.
3. **Which channels are overrated.** A channel that looks strong in analytics but never appears in what customers say may be collecting credit for decisions made elsewhere.

Review this monthly, and change one thing at a time so you can see the effect.

## Mistakes to avoid

**Trusting small numbers.** Ten answers are a hint, not a trend. Wait until you have enough answers for patterns to repeat before moving budget.

**Treating it as exact.** People forget, simplify and give the most recent reason. Use the answers to understand direction, not to calculate precise returns.

**Asking for more than you need.** The question is about how they found you, nothing more. Do not use it as a reason to collect extra personal details, and make sure your privacy notice covers what you collect.

**Letting it rot.** The answers lose value if nobody reads them. Put the weekly sorting on someone's calendar.

## A small change with a long life

Adding one question to a form takes an afternoon. Reading the answers takes half an hour a week. In return, a startup gets a view of where its customers really come from, in their own words. For a team deciding where to spend limited time and money, that is a good trade.
