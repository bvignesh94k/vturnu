# Who holds the keys to your company's website? Keep a register before you need one

**By B. Vignesh, Founder & CEO, VTurnU**

Ask a simple question at your next management meeting: who can log in to the account where our domain name is registered? If the answer is a pause, this article is for you.

A business website looks like one thing. It is really a chain of separate accounts: the domain, the DNS, the hosting, the content management system, the company email, the analytics, the advertising accounts and the social pages. Each one was opened by somebody, at some point, with some email address. Sometimes that somebody was a freelancer, an agency or an employee who has since moved on.

Nothing goes wrong until the day it does. The site needs an urgent change, a renewal fails, a supplier relationship ends badly or a staff member leaves. Then the business discovers that it does not hold the keys to its own front door.

## Renewal notices go to whoever is on the record

Domain names show why this matters. Under ICANN's [Expired Registration Recovery Policy](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/registrant-about-errp-2018-12-07-en), registrars of generic domains such as .com must send the registered name holder two renewal reminders, roughly a month and a week before the expiry date, and at least one more after it.

Those reminders go to the contact details on the registration. If that is the inbox of a former web designer, your business may never see them. Country domains such as .co.za, .ng or .ke are run under their own registry rules, but the lesson is the same: the notice goes to the address on record.

## The register: one page, eight lines

I suggest every business keeps a simple digital asset register. It needs eight lines:

1. Domain name registrar
2. DNS, which decides where the domain points
3. Website hosting
4. Content management system, with a list of administrators
5. Company email platform
6. Analytics and search console accounts
7. Advertising accounts
8. Social media pages and business listings

For each line, record five things: which provider the account is with, who the legal owner is, which email address controls it, who else has administrator access and when it renews.

## Five rules that make the register worth having

- **The business owns every account.** Suppliers get access. They do not get ownership. If an agency opened an account in its own name, ask for it to be transferred now, while the relationship is good.
- **Use a company address, not a person.** An address such as webmaster@ or it@ on your own domain, read by two people, survives resignations.
- **Keep two administrators on everything.** One person on leave should never mean a locked account.
- **Turn on two-step verification.** Store the passwords in a password manager, not in a spreadsheet or in someone's memory.
- **Put renewal dates in a shared calendar.** Switch on auto-renew, and check that the card on file has not expired.

## Write the exit into the contract

I run a digital agency myself, and I would rather agree the handover on day one than argue about it on the last day. Add one clause to every agreement with a web, IT or marketing supplier: on termination, the supplier hands over all logins, files and data within an agreed number of days, and removes its own access. It costs nothing to agree at the start. It is far harder to negotiate at the end.

The register matters most when you change suppliers or rebuild the site, because that is when accounts get moved, copied or forgotten. If a rebuild is on your list, my guide to [redesigning a website without losing search traffic](https://vturnu.com/blog/website-redesign-without-losing-seo/) covers the technical side.

## Review it every quarter

Set a reminder every three months to open the register and test it. Can two people still log in to each account? Has anyone left who still has access? Is anything due for renewal?

The exercise takes an afternoon the first time and half an hour after that. Compare that with a website that stays offline because nobody can reach the account.

A website is a business asset. Treat its keys the way you treat the keys to the office: know how many there are, and who holds them.
